Before you start: please read

This Part covers years many people remember. Some of the people in it, and many of their families, are living. It includes the long fight for rights and land, and, near the end, the deaths of Aboriginal people in custody. That section names no one who died, describes no deaths, and shows no images of the people who died.

Some old documents use words that are offensive today. They are quoted as they were written. It is all right to stop. If anything here is upsetting, tell your teacher or a trusted adult, or talk to one of these services. They are free and confidential.

In 1943 a hospital ship was sunk off Queensland. After the war, Aboriginal people were made citizens on paper, and given the Commonwealth vote in 1962. The Yolŋu sent bark petitions to Parliament. The Gurindji walked off Wave Hill. Voters changed the Constitution. Four men set up an embassy under a beach umbrella. And a Royal Commission asked why so many Aboriginal people had died in custody.

How this works

Map the years from 1943 to 1991. Compare what the laws and petitions said with what the other records show. Decode the words. Then investigate four questions. In each one, read what is agreed, ask three witnesses the same three questions, and write your finding.

What did they say? Could they really know? Why might they say it? “We can’t know” is always an allowed answer, if you can say why.

    Citizens by birth, 1948

    What the paper said

    Nationality and Citizenship Act 1948 · section 10

    “10.—(1.) Subject to this section, a person born in Australia after the commencement of this Act shall be an Australian citizen by birth.”

    Source. Nationality and Citizenship Act 1948 (Cth), No. 83 of 1948, s. 10.

    See the source (Documenting a Democracy) ↗

    What the records say happened

    The select committee · 1961

    A select committee of the Commonwealth Parliament took evidence from 324 people, almost half of them Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander. It found that 30,000 Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people in the Northern Territory, Western Australia and Queensland were not allowed to vote.

    Source. National Museum of Australia, Digital Classroom, “Indigenous Australians granted right to vote” (secondhand; the committee’s report was not seen). A summary, not a quotation.

    Section 10 made Aboriginal people born in Australia citizens. Did citizenship bring the vote?

    Does the record match the paper?

    The right to vote, 1962

    What the paper said

    Commonwealth Electoral Act 1962 · long title

    “An Act to give to Aboriginal Natives of Australia the right to Enrol and to Vote as Electors of the Commonwealth, and to provide for certain Offences in relation thereto.”

    Source. Commonwealth Electoral Act 1962 (Cth), No. 31 of 1962, long title. Federal Register of Legislation, as made.

    See the source (Federal Register of Legislation) ↗

    What the records say happened

    The same Act · section 3, adding sub-section (5) to section 42 (compulsory enrolment)

    “(5.) This section does not apply to a person who is an aboriginal native of Australia except to the extent that such a person may, if he so chooses, comply with sub-section (1.) of this section.”

    Source. Commonwealth Electoral Act 1962, s. 3.

    See the source ↗

    What came later

    From 1984 Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people were required to enrol and vote in the same way as other voters.

    Source. NMA Digital Classroom. A summary, not a quotation.

    The title gave “the right to Enrol and to Vote”. Were Aboriginal voters now treated like every other voter?

    Yirrkala, 1963

    What the paper said

    The Yirrkala bark petition · point 7 · August 1963

    “7. And they humbly pray that the Honourable the House of Representatives will appoint a Committee, accompanied by competent interpreters, to hear the views of the people of Yirrkala before permitting the excision of this land.”

    Source. Petition of the Yirrkala people to the House of Representatives, 1963, English text. Documenting a Democracy. Copyright: permission required.

    See the transcript (Documenting a Democracy) ↗

    What the records say happened

    What happened

    A parliamentary committee heard evidence at Yirrkala and in Darwin. On 29 October 1963 it recommended compensation for loss of livelihood, protection of sacred sites, and a parliamentary committee to watch over the mining. When their appeals to Parliament failed, the Yolŋu leaders went to court. In 1971 the Supreme Court of the Northern Territory held that Australian law could not recognise the Yolŋu relationship to the land as property.

    Source. Museum of Australian Democracy, Documenting a Democracy, history of the petitions. A summary, not a quotation.

    The petition asked for a committee to hear the people “before permitting the excision”. Did the committee stop it?

    Does the record match the paper?

    Citizens, 1944 to 1962

    Did citizenship make Aboriginal people equal citizens?

    Draft, pending consultation

    What is agreed

    • In 1944 Western Australia passed the Natives (Citizenship Rights) Act. An Aboriginal person could apply for a certificate of citizenship (National Museum of Australia).
    • Until 1949 there was no such thing as an Australian citizen. People born in Australia were British subjects. The Nationality and Citizenship Act 1948 created Australian citizenship.
    • State Acts that governed where Aboriginal people could live, work and marry stayed in force, and so did section 4 of the Franchise Act of 1902.
    • On 21 May 1962 the Commonwealth Electoral Act received assent.

    The National Museum of Australia

    On the 1944 WA Act · a modern summary

    Source. National Museum of Australia, Digital Classroom, “Indigenous Australians granted right to vote”. Secondhand: the 1944 Act itself was not seen.

    See the source (NMA) ↗

    What did they say they saw? The 1944 Act, it says, “allowed Aboriginal people to vote only if they could speak English, had ‘industrious habits’ (worked hard) and didn’t have certain medical conditions”. Aboriginal people called it the dog-collar act.

    Could they really have seen it? A national museum, writing for students, decades later. It summarises the Act; it does not quote it.

    Why might they say it? It was written to explain the history to students. It includes how Aboriginal people saw the law, not just what the law said.

    The Nationality and Citizenship Act

    Section 10 · 1948

    Source. Nationality and Citizenship Act 1948 (Cth), No. 83 of 1948, s. 10.

    See the source (Documenting a Democracy) ↗

    What did they say they saw? “10.—(1.) Subject to this section, a person born in Australia after the commencement of this Act shall be an Australian citizen by birth.”

    Could they really have seen it? It is the law. It names no race. It says nothing about voting.

    Why might they say it? It created a new citizenship for everyone born here. Read what it leaves out as closely as what it says.

    Long title, and section 3 · 21 May 1962

    Source. Commonwealth Electoral Act 1962 (Cth), No. 31 of 1962. Federal Register of Legislation, as made.

    The Yirrkala Bark Petitions, 1963

    What did the Yolŋu ask Parliament for, and what did they get?

    Draft, pending consultation

    About the petitions

    The petitions are framed by bark paintings that carry Yolŋu law. They are not reproduced here. Their images belong to the Yolŋu clans concerned and need permission. You can see them on the Museum of Australian Democracy’s page.

    What is agreed

    • On 13 March 1963 the Commonwealth removed more than 300 square kilometres from the Arnhem Land reserve so that bauxite could be mined. Work started without talking to the Yolŋu people about their land.
    • In August the Yolŋu sent petitions to the House of Representatives, typed in Yolŋu Matha and in English and framed by bark paintings. They were presented on 14 and 28 August.
    • On 29 October 1963 a parliamentary committee recommended compensation for loss of livelihood, protection of sacred sites, and a committee to watch over the mining.
    • In 1968 the Yolŋu took their claim to the Supreme Court of the Northern Territory. The judge gave his decision in 1971.

    The Yirrkala bark petition · August 1963 · English text

    Source. Petition of the Yirrkala people to the House of Representatives, 1963. Documenting a Democracy. Copyright: permission required.

    Wave Hill, 1966 to 1975

    Was the walk-off about wages, or about land?

    Draft, pending consultation

    What is agreed

    • The Gurindji people have lived in the Victoria River country of the Northern Territory for tens of thousands of years. In 1883 the colonial government leased almost 3,000 square kilometres of their country to a pastoralist.
    • In 1953 the Northern Territory set wage and ration standards for Aboriginal workers. The wages were sometimes less than half of those paid to white workers for the same work.
    • On 23 August 1966 about two hundred Gurindji stockmen, domestic workers and their families walked off Wave Hill station. Their spokesman was Vincent Lingiari.
    • On 16 August 1975 the Prime Minister, Gough Whitlam, handed Lingiari deeds to part of Gurindji country at Daguragu.

    The Gurindji petition

    To the Governor-General · April 1967

    Source. “The Original Wave Hill Mob Letter of 1967”, Aboriginal Law Bulletin (1986). The original is held by the National Archives of Australia, Darwin. Copyright: permission required.

    See the source (AustLII) ↗About the petition (NMA) ↗

    What did they say they saw? “We, the leaders of the Gurindji people, write to you about our earnest desire to regain tenure of our tribal lands in the Wave Hill - Limbunya area of the Northern Territory, of which we were dispossessed in time past, and for which we received no recompense.”

    The Constitution, before

    Sections 51(xxvi) and 127 · 1900

    Source. Commonwealth of Australia Constitution Act 1900 (UK), ss. 51(xxvi), 127.

    See the source (Documenting a Democracy) ↗

    What did they say they saw? “(xxvi) The people of any race, other than the aboriginal race in any State, for whom it is deemed necessary to make special laws”

    “In reckoning the numbers of the people of the Commonwealth, or of a State or other part of the Commonwealth, aboriginal natives shall not be counted.”

    Could they really have seen it? It is the founding law, as written in the 1890s and in force until 1967.

    Why might they say it? These two parts left Aboriginal people out: out of the Commonwealth’s power to make laws for a race, and out of the count.

    The Constitution Alteration

    Long title and section 3 · 1967

    Source. Constitution Alteration (Aboriginals) 1967 (Cth), No. 55 of 1967. Assented to 10 August 1967.

    See the source (Federal Register of Legislation) ↗

    What did they say they saw? “An Act to alter the Constitution so as to omit certain words relating to the People of the Aboriginal Race in any State and so that Aboriginals are to be counted in reckoning the Population.”

    “3. The Constitution is altered by repealing section 127.”

    Could they really have seen it? It is the proposed law the voters approved. It says exactly what it removes.

    Why might they say it? It removes words. It does not add any new right in words of its own.

    The Gazette

    Statement of the result · 26 June 1967

    Source. Commonwealth of Australia Gazette, No. 55, 26 June 1967.

    See the source (Gazette) ↗

    What did they say they saw?

    In favour 5,183,113 · not in favour 527,007. Every State voted in favour. Western Australia had the lowest yes vote: 319,823 in favour, 75,282 not in favour.

    Could they really have seen it? It is the official count, certified by the Chief Electoral Officer. Under the law it was “conclusive evidence” of the result.

    1972 to 1991, in brief

    Two more chapters of Part Two, told through the records without witnesses or findings.

    The Tent Embassy, 26 January 1972

    On 26 January 1972 the Prime Minister, William McMahon, issued a statement on Aboriginal policy. Aboriginal groups on reserves in the Northern Territory could apply for a new kind of lease, for up to fifty years. The statement explained why the Government had not chosen another course.

    William McMahon · statement · 26 January 1972

    “We decided to create this new form of lease rather than attempt simply to translate the Aboriginal affinity with the land into some form of legal right under the Australian system” …

    Source. Statement by the Prime Minister, 26 January 1972. Indigenous Law Resources, AustLII.

    See the source (AustLII) ↗

    The same day four men, Billy Craigie, Tony Coorey, Michael Anderson and Bertie Williams, came to Canberra from Sydney.

    Parliamentary Education Office

    “They established the ‘Aboriginal embassy’ under a beach umbrella at the front of Parliament House (now known as Old Parliament House).”

    Source. Parliamentary Education Office, “Aboriginal Tent Embassy”.

    See the source (PEO) ↗

    Deaths in Custody, 1987 to 1991

    Care

    This section is about Aboriginal people who died in custody. It gives only the Royal Commission’s own words and numbers. No one who died is named or shown. If this is upsetting, stop, and talk to your teacher, or call 13YARN on 13 92 76.

    On 10 August 1987 the Prime Minister, Bob Hawke, announced a Royal Commission into the deaths of Aboriginal people in custody. Elliott Johnston was appointed to lead it in 1989. His final report was signed on 15 April 1991 and made 339 recommendations.

    Royal Commission into Aboriginal Deaths in Custody · National Report · vol. 1 · 1991

    “This report would not have been made but for the deaths of ninety-nine Aboriginal men and women who died in Australian police, prison and juvenile detention custody between 1 January 1980 and 31 May 1989.”

    Source. RCIADIC, National Report (1991), vol. 1, acknowledgements. Indigenous Law Resources, AustLII.

    See the source (AustLII) ↗

    Royal Commission · National Report · vol. 1 · 1991

    “Commissioners identified the principal and immediate explanation for the deaths in custody as being the disproportionate rate at which Aboriginal people are detained, arrested and imprisoned in Australia.”
    … “the grossly disproportionate rates at which Aboriginal people are taken into custody, of the order of more than twenty times the rate for non-Aboriginals.”

    Source. RCIADIC, National Report (1991), vol. 1. Indigenous Law Resources, AustLII.
